\section{Experimental Methodology and Datasets}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Device and Error Definition}
We use a Pupil Labs Neon wearable eye tracker, which estimates gaze at 200\,Hz from two eye cameras without user calibration and records a front-facing scene camera\footnote{\todo{confirm scene camera: 1600$\times$1200\,px, 30\,Hz, 103\dg$\times$77\dg{} field of view}}, eye-image streams and an IMU. All analyses use the uncorrected device gaze: wearer-specific offsets stored in the vendor app were removed before processing. Gaze samples are synchronised to scene frames and converted, like the target reference points, to azimuth/elevation directions using the camera intrinsics (positive right/up). The \emph{angular error} of a sample is the spherical angle between gaze and target direction; the \emph{bias} is the componentwise gaze-minus-target difference. Each participant is analysed separately and no samples are pooled across people. Participants are pseudonymised as P1--P6.

\subsection{Real-World Driving Benchmark}
\textbf{Acquisition.} Two qualified drivers of our research vehicle took part. In the evaluated recordings, P2 drove and P1 rode in the front passenger seat\todo{confirm roles}. Sixteen recordings (1.2\,h) on three days cover urban roads (P1) and suburban roads and highway (P2). The vehicle records camera, LiDAR and GNSS data; Neon and vehicle clocks are aligned with measured clock offsets or manual frame anchors. The participants drive naturally and need no calibration targets: whenever they deliberately fixate an object, such as a licence plate, traffic light or road sign, they say so (e.g., ``fixating stop sign'').

\textbf{Gaze-guided, human-in-the-loop annotation.} A semi-automatic pipeline turns these recordings into reference observations without annotating frames from scratch (Fig.~\ref{fig:framework}, top). (i)~Voice detection converts each utterance into an event with a timestamp and the announced object category\todo{name speech detector}. (ii)~Around the measured gaze position, a search region a few times larger than the expected gaze error\todo{radius} restricts object detection to the announced category. (iii)~If exactly one object of the announced category lies in the search region, it is assigned automatically and its box is propagated over the fixation by bidirectional optical-flow template tracking. (iv)~As soon as several candidates are present, e.g., multiple licence plates, the scene is flagged for manual review instead of being assigned. (v)~In a dedicated Neon Player plugin, annotators inspect gaze, detections, tracks and assignments, and confirm, correct or manually annotate the target. Automation proposes candidates, and human review decides: \emph{every} target in the benchmark has been manually validated. Recordings, voice events, detections and validated assignments remain linked for later verification.\todo{report share of automatic assignments accepted unchanged, flagged, and annotated manually}

\textbf{Independent references.} Gaze only restricts where the detector searches; it never chooses between candidates. The reference point is the centre of the validated object box in the scene image and is therefore independent of the gaze sample it is compared with. The benchmark contains 152 validated scenes (P1: 79, P2: 73) with 53{,}345 valid gaze samples; median scene durations are 2.5\,s (P1) and 0.6\,s (P2).

\textbf{Scene metadata.} Each scene carries the target class and motion, ego motion, relative motion between ego vehicle and target, road type and the viewing region (150 of 152 scenes through the windshield, the others through a side window). Target distance comes from camera--LiDAR fusion for 93 scenes and from a camera-based estimate otherwise (median 23.5\,m for P1 and 35.3\,m for P2, range 6.7--106.5\,m); ego speed comes from GNSS for 98 scenes and from annotation otherwise. Each sample additionally carries eye-image exposure statistics, IMU signals, pupil and eyelid measures and gaze velocity, which serve as quality descriptors.

\subsection{Controlled Dataset}
The controlled dataset contains 216 recordings (2.9\,h) from six participants. Static targets are the 15 inner corners of 5$\times$3 chessboards shown on a screen with bright or dark background or printed, fixated sequentially in forward or reverse order at 1, 2 and 4\,m, indoors and outdoors. A smooth-pursuit protocol shows a target moving along a fixed route on the screen. P1 completed all protocols over several days (170 recordings), P2 the static and distance protocols (23 recordings); P3--P6 contributed 3--7 static screen recordings each, in bright and, except P4, dark conditions. For each target, the central 60\% of the fixation interval is analysed.

\subsection{Metrics}
Following~\cite{niehorster2026fundamentals7}, we separate four quantities: \emph{accuracy}, the median angular error of a fixation or scene; \emph{bias}, the median signed azimuth and elevation offset; \emph{dispersion}, $P_\mathrm{STD}=\sqrt{\sigma^2_\mathrm{az}+\sigma^2_\mathrm{el}}$ of the gaze samples (for pursuit, of gaze minus target, so that target motion is not counted as noise); and \emph{precision}, the RMS of sample-to-sample angular steps (RMS-S2S). Driving scores are weighted so that each test recording, each scene within a recording and each sample within a scene contribute equally; we report the weighted median and 90th percentile (P90) of held-out errors, averaged over splits.

\subsection{Correction Protocols}
\textbf{Constant offsets.} A correction subtracts the componentwise median of the selected reference biases from all gaze samples. \emph{Chessboard} offsets are estimated from one board (15 corners) or from all training boards pooled within a five-fold split of the controlled recordings, and are applied unchanged to the driving scenes. \emph{Driving} offsets are estimated from driving scenes: per participant, scenes are shuffled into five blocks, and each block in turn serves as calibration set ($\approx$20\% of scenes) while the remaining blocks are test scenes. Within each split we compare the pooled calibration scenes, single scenes, random subsets of $k\in\{1,\dots,8\}$ scenes (20 draws) and geometric layouts (lines, triangles, rectangles) selected from the calibration-side gaze positions. No scene is used for calibration and test in the same split, but scenes of the same drive can appear on both sides.

\textbf{Extended person-specific correction.} The extended correction replaces the raw gaze signal $\mathbf{g}(t)$ by a causal signal $\tilde{\mathbf{g}}(t)$ (raw, delayed by 10--40\,ms, or a trailing median over 50 or 100\,ms) and optionally adds a position term, a linear function of gaze position fitted with ridge regularisation ($\alpha\in\{1,10\}$) to the calibration-scene residuals: $\hat{\mathbf{g}}(t)=\tilde{\mathbf{g}}(t)-\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{B}\,\tilde{\mathbf{g}}(t)$, with $\mathbf{b}$ the median offset. For each participant and split, one of the 21 candidates is selected by leave-one-scene-out evaluation on the calibration scenes only and then scored once on the test scenes. We vary the calibration share between 20\% and 80\% (16 random shufflings at 20\%, five otherwise). The method is exploratory: it was designed after a per-sample ridge regression on gaze position, velocity and target distance failed to beat the baseline (Sec.~\ref{sec:transfer}).

\textbf{Statistics.} Controlled factors are tested within participant with Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for paired units, Kruskal--Wallis tests across levels and Spearman correlation for drift, with Holm correction per participant and factor and bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals. Driving comparisons use paired Wilcoxon tests on per-scene errors averaged over all test appearances (Holm-corrected); scene subsets with at least six scenes are tested with Benjamini--Hochberg correction per participant and calibration share.
